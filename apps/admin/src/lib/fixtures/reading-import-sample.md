Passage 1: The Glassmakers of Murano

A. For more than seven centuries the small island of Murano, a short boat ride from Venice, has been synonymous with fine glass. Its reputation began in 1291, when the Venetian authorities ordered every glass furnace in the city to move to the island. The official reason was the danger of fire in a city built largely of timber, but the move also made it far easier to keep the craft's secrets inside a single, closely watched community.

B. Those secrets were valuable. Murano's workshops perfected clear glass, known as cristallo, by purifying the ash of coastal plants and carefully controlling the heat of their furnaces. They also developed techniques such as millefiori, in which thin rods of coloured glass are sliced and arranged into patterns, and lattimo, an opaque white glass made to imitate porcelain.

C. Glassmakers enjoyed unusual privileges. Their daughters were permitted to marry into noble families, and the most skilled masters were treated almost as minor aristocrats. Yet they were also forbidden to leave the Republic. A craftsman who tried to set up a workshop abroad could have his family imprisoned, and some historians claim that agents were sent to bring deserters home.

D. Today the industry faces quieter threats. Rising energy prices have made the gas-fired furnaces expensive to run, and cheap imitations sold to tourists undermine the value of genuine work. Several family firms have closed in the past decade. Others have responded by collaborating with contemporary designers, and a regional trademark now certifies glass that was made on the island.

Passage 2: Sleep and the Teenage Brain

**A.** It is a familiar complaint of parents that teenagers stay up late and cannot be woken in the morning. For a long time this was put down to poor habits. Research over the past thirty years suggests instead that adolescent body clocks genuinely shift, so that the hormone melatonin is released up to two hours later than in younger children.

**B.** The consequences are measurable. A study of high-school students in Minnesota found that when the start of the school day was moved from 7.15 to 8.40, average sleep increased by almost an hour. Attendance improved, and the number of car accidents involving teenage drivers fell sharply.

**C.** Not everyone is convinced. Some head teachers argue that later starts simply push the rest of the day later, so that after-school sports and part-time jobs end in the dark. Bus companies, too, have objected that staggered timetables are expensive to run.

**D.** Sleep researchers respond that the benefits outweigh the costs. Dr Ana Reyes, who has followed several districts through the change, points out that tired students learn less, whatever time the bell rings. Professor Lee Hart adds that the effect is strongest for students from poorer families.

**E.** Screens are often blamed as well. Light from phones and tablets can delay melatonin further, and messages arriving late at night keep the mind alert. However, Dr Sam Okafor warns against treating devices as the whole story; in his surveys, even teenagers without phones slept later than their parents.

Passage 3: Urban Beekeeping

A. Beehives have become a common sight on city rooftops. In London alone the number of registered hives has more than doubled in a decade, and hotels, banks and supermarkets now advertise their own honey. The trend is usually presented as good news for nature, a small way for city dwellers to help struggling pollinators.

B. I am not so sure. Honeybees are managed livestock, not wild animals, and they compete for nectar and pollen with hundreds of species of wild bee. Where hives are crowded together, the flowers within flying distance can be stripped bare, leaving little for solitary bees that cannot travel far.

C. The process of producing honey is itself fascinating. Foragers collect nectar and store it in a special stomach. Back at the hive, workers pass it from mouth to mouth, adding enzymes. The liquid is then spread in the cells of the comb, where the bees fan their wings to evaporate water until it is thick enough to store, and finally they seal each cell with wax.

D. None of this means city beekeeping should be banned. But planting more flowers would do far more for pollinators than adding more hives, and councils should consider limiting the number of hives in each area. Done carefully, a few rooftop colonies can coexist with the wild bees they are supposed to help.
